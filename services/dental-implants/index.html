01 — The basics

What a dental implant actually is

A small, screw-shaped titanium post placed into the jawbone where a tooth root used to be. Over the following months the bone grows directly onto its surface — a process called osseointegration. Once that bond is solid, the implant carries chewing force the way a natural root does.

A finished implant has three parts:

  • Fixture — the post itself, hidden under the gum and integrated with bone.
  • Abutment — the connector passing through the gum that holds the restoration.
  • Restoration — the crown, bridge or denture you see and chew with.

Because the post transmits pressure into bone, the jaw stays stimulated. Bridges and conventional dentures rest on top of the gum instead, which is why the ridge beneath them slowly resorbs and dentures need relining every few years.

02 — Treatment options

Four ways we restore missing teeth

The right option depends on how many teeth are missing, how much bone is available, and what you want day to day. Every plan starts from the same 3D scan.

SI-01®

Single-Tooth Implant

One post, one crown. Replaces a single missing tooth without touching the healthy teeth beside it — the main advantage over a three-unit bridge, which requires filing down two intact teeth to serve as anchors.

BEST FOR
One missing or failing tooth with adequate bone

IB-02®

Implant-Supported Bridge

Two or more implants carry a bridge spanning several missing teeth in a row. Fewer implants than teeth replaced, which keeps cost and surgical time down while still anchoring everything into bone.

BEST FOR
Three or more adjacent missing teeth

FA-04™ Same-day teeth

All-on-4 Full Arch

A complete fixed arch on four to six implants, two of them angled to use the denser bone at the front of the jaw. In most cases a provisional fixed bridge goes on the same day — you do not leave without teeth.

BEST FOR
A failing or fully edentulous arch

OD-02™

Implant-Retained Overdenture

A removable denture that clips onto two to four implants instead of relying on suction or adhesive. Considerably cheaper than a fixed arch, and it stops the lower denture lifting when you talk or eat.

BEST FOR
Denture wearers who want stability

03 — Why implants

What implants do that bridges and dentures cannot

Bone is preserved

Bone that loses its root starts resorbing within months. An implant keeps loading the bone, slowing that loss and protecting your facial profile.

Neighbours stay intact

Unlike a conventional bridge, nothing is ground down. Adjacent teeth keep their enamel and their own long-term prognosis.

You chew normally

Fixed implant teeth restore most natural bite force — steak, apples and corn go back on the menu. Conventional dentures deliver a fraction of it.

Built to last

Long-term studies put implant survival near 95% at ten years. Crowns may need replacing eventually; the integrated post usually does not.

No adhesives, no slipping

Fixed restorations do not move when you speak or laugh, and there is no paste to reapply through the day.

They clean like teeth

Brush, clean between them daily, keep your hygiene visits. No soaking cup, no separate routine.

04 — Protocol

The implant process, start to finish

Most of the calendar time is healing, not appointments. A straightforward single implant usually means three or four visits spread over several months.

  1. Visit 1 · 45–60 min

    Consultation and 3D scan

    We take a cone-beam CT scan and a digital impression, review your medical history and medications, and measure bone height, width, and the position of the sinus and nerve. You leave with a written treatment plan and an itemised estimate.

  2. If needed · 3–6 months

    Extraction, bone graft or sinus lift

    If the tooth is still in place it comes out, usually with a socket-preservation graft at the same appointment. Thin ridges are widened; upper back teeth sometimes need a sinus lift to create height. Not everyone needs this stage.

  3. Visit 2 · 60–90 min

    Implant placement

    Using a surgical guide printed from your scan, the surgeon places the post through a small opening in the gum. You are numb throughout, with nitrous oxide or IV sedation available. Most patients describe it as easier than the extraction that preceded it.

  4. 3–6 months

    Osseointegration

    The bone fuses to the implant surface. You wear a temporary tooth, flipper or provisional bridge meanwhile — nobody is sent away with a visible gap in the smile zone. Progress is checked at short appointments.

  5. Visit 3 · 30 min

    Abutment and final impression

    Once the implant is stable we attach the abutment and scan for the final restoration. Shade is matched to your neighbouring teeth in natural light.

  6. Visit 4 · 30–45 min

    Delivery of the final tooth

    The crown, bridge or fixed arch is fitted, the bite adjusted, and you go back to eating normally. We see you at six months, then at your regular hygiene visits.

05 — Candidacy

Are you a candidate?

Most adults with a missing or failing tooth are. The scan settles it — bone volume is the single biggest factor, and grafting solves most shortfalls.

Usually a good fit

  • Adult with fully developed jaw growth
  • Healthy gums, or gum disease treated and stabilised
  • Enough bone, or willingness to have a graft first
  • Non-smoker, or able to stop through healing
  • Committed to daily hygiene and regular checkups

Needs a closer look first

Uncontrolled diabetes, active periodontal disease, heavy smoking, head and neck radiation, high-dose IV bisphosphonates and untreated bruxism all raise the risk of failure. None is an automatic no — they change sequencing, and sometimes we coordinate with your physician before booking surgery.

Bone grafting

“I was told I don’t have enough bone.”

One of the most common things we hear, and rarely the end of the conversation. Options include:

  • Socket preservation — grafting at extraction so the ridge does not collapse.
  • Ridge augmentation — rebuilding width or height before placement.
  • Sinus lift — raising the sinus floor to gain height in the upper back jaw.
  • Angled placement — using available bone instead of building new bone.

Grafts typically heal 3–6 months before the implant goes in. Some are done at the same appointment as placement.

06 — Science

Planned digitally. Placed precisely.

Every case is built in software before an instrument is picked up. The scan sets the angle, the depth, and the safety margin around the nerve — surgery becomes execution of a plan rather than a judgement call in the moment.

Book your 3D scan

Cone-beam CT

A low-dose 3D scan showing bone density and the exact position of the sinus and inferior alveolar nerve — detail a flat X-ray cannot give.

Guided surgery

The plan is printed as a surgical guide that fixes drill angle and depth. Smaller openings, less guesswork, faster healing.

Digital impressions

An intraoral scanner replaces trays and putty, and the file goes straight to the lab — better fitting restorations, fewer remakes.

08 — Recovery

The first week, and the next twenty years

Discomfort after a single implant is usually mild and peaks on day two. Most patients manage with over-the-counter ibuprofen and are back at work the next day.

First 48 hours

  • Ice 20 minutes on, 20 off, to limit swelling
  • Soft foods — nothing hot, crunchy or spicy
  • No smoking, straws or vigorous rinsing
  • Rest — skip the gym for two or three days

Keeping an implant healthy long term

Implants do not decay, but the gum and bone around them can become inflamed — a condition called peri-implantitis, and the leading cause of late implant failure. It is largely preventable.

  • Brush twice daily; clean between teeth every day
  • Water flossers and interdental brushes work well around implants
  • Professional cleanings every six months — every three or four with a history of gum disease
  • Wear a night guard if you grind; bruxism is hard on implant restorations
  • Call early about bleeding, soreness, or a restoration that feels loose

10 — Questions

Implant FAQ

Still unsure? Bring the question to the consultation — it is free.

Ask us directly

Does getting an implant hurt?

The placement itself should not hurt — the area is fully numb, and sedation is available if you would rather be unaware of the procedure. Afterwards most patients report soreness comparable to an extraction, peaking around day two and controlled with ibuprofen. Full-arch surgery involves more swelling and a longer soft-food phase.

How long does the whole process take?

For a single implant with adequate bone, roughly three to six months from placement to final crown — most of it waiting for bone to fuse. Add three to six months if you need a graft or sinus lift first. Full-arch cases often get fixed provisional teeth the same day, with the final bridge four to six months later.

How long do dental implants last?

The titanium post frequently lasts for life; published survival rates sit around 95% at ten years. The visible restoration is the part that wears — crowns commonly need replacing somewhere between 10 and 20 years. Hygiene and smoking status matter more than any other variable.

Can my body reject a dental implant?

Titanium is biologically inert, so true rejection in the immune sense is essentially unheard of. Failures happen for mechanical or infectious reasons instead: the implant does not integrate, bacteria inflame the surrounding tissue, or the post is overloaded. Titanium allergy exists but is rare; zirconia implants are an alternative when it is documented.

Am I too old for implants?

There is no upper age limit — healing capacity and medical status matter, not the number. We place implants routinely for patients in their seventies and eighties. Being too young is the real constraint: the jaw needs to have finished growing, generally the late teens or early twenties.

What is the difference between All-on-4 and regular dentures?

A conventional denture rests on the gums, is removed nightly, and delivers a fraction of natural bite force. All-on-4 screws a fixed bridge onto four or more implants — you cannot take it out, it does not move, and it preserves bone. The trade-off is cost and a surgical procedure.

Will insurance cover my implant?

Coverage varies a lot. Plans that exclude the implant fixture often still cover the extraction, the bone graft, or the crown. We verify your benefits in writing before treatment starts and tell you the out-of-pocket figure up front.

What happens if I do nothing about a missing tooth?

The bone in that site resorbs — fastest in the first year. Neighbouring teeth drift into the gap and the opposing tooth over-erupts, which changes your bite and makes later treatment harder and more expensive. Waiting is the option that most often turns a simple case into a complex one.
